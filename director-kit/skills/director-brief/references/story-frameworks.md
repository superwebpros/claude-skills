# Story Frameworks — Reference

Operable story/character/structure principles for turning a marketing goal into a compelling short narrative brief. Distilled from the 1-Week Director course (Days 1–2). End use: 15–45 second video ads. Pull what you need; every section ends with a **→ For a short-form ad/reel** note.

**First principle (everything serves this):** the only job is to *hook and move the audience* — never "look cool." Every choice filters through "how does this make the viewer feel?" not "is this clever?" A story is a tool for living: it shows someone *want* something they lack, get beaten by conflict, change, and become big enough to win. A character who wants nothing, acts on nothing, and never changes is not a story — it is death (and dead ads).

---

## 1. Story Structure — The 5 Essential Elements (reusable template)

Every story, any length, needs all five. ~75% of beginners omit them. Fill in:

- [ ] **Character** — who (can be anything; a talking pencil works)
- [ ] **Wants something** — a clear, explicit desire → this opens a *mental loop* in the viewer ("did they get it?") they can't close until the end. Like a text left on read. This is the hook.
- [ ] **Takes action** to get it — story is told through ACTION, not narration or dialogue
- [ ] **Meets conflict** — no conflict, no story
- [ ] **Climax + resolution** — change occurs (protagonist changes, or forces change in another)

**The connective-tissue rule (the structure test core):** Beats must connect with **"BUT"** and **"THEREFORE"**, never **"AND THEN."** "And then" = an IKEA instruction list that bores viewers. "But/therefore" = cause-and-effect momentum.
- ❌ This happens, AND THEN this, AND THEN this.
- ✅ This happens, BUT that blocks it, THEREFORE they must do this.

**Escalating stakes rule:** every beat must risk MORE than the one before. If a beat lowers the stakes, the story is circling, not building — rework it.

**Change rule:** characters change only under the pressure of conflict. Shorter film = less pronounced change, but it must still register.

**→ For a short-form ad/reel:** Under ~30s the only hard requirement is *be interesting* — but interesting still means a want + a loop opened fast. Compress to one want, one obstacle, one turn. The "but/therefore" rule is what keeps the **middle from sagging**: an ad hooks on the want, then dies if the middle is "and then we show feature, and then another feature." Force a **BUT** right after the hook (the obstacle/objection) and a **THEREFORE** into the payoff. That single but/therefore hinge is the anti-sag mechanism.

## 2. Character — Make It Active, and Character = Structure

**Plot vs. character is a false debate. Character IS structure.** A character's choices under pressure create the cause-and-effect chain that *is* the plot. They are inseparable.

- **Character** = the choices a person makes *under pressure*. The greater the pressure, the more the choice reveals true nature.
- **Characterization** = observable surface traits (job, age, dress, smarts, strength). These exist only to make the choices *believable* (old/young, smart/dumb, weak/strong enough that the choice is plausible).
- Choices with no risk are meaningless. Pressure is mandatory.

**The #1 beginner failure: the passive protagonist** — a ragdoll that events happen *to*. Poison for an audience.
- **Active protagonist** takes deliberate action that puts them in direct conflict with their world.
- **The Decision Test (apply to every beat):** *Did the protagonist cause this event, or did it happen to them?* If it happens to them → rework so an earlier choice causes it.
- Passive is allowed ONLY at the very start. After the inciting incident they may decline briefly, but rising heat must force commitment — then no more passivity.
- Each action → the world responds *more powerfully and differently than expected* → they must recalibrate and act bigger/riskier. Repeat to climax.

**The Risk Test (validates stakes):** *What does the protagonist stand to lose? What's the worst thing that happens if they fail?* If the answer isn't compelling, rework the stakes.

**→ For a short-form ad/reel:** Cast the *viewer's stand-in* (the prospect) as the active protagonist — they want a result and must DO something, not get sold to. One choice under pressure beats three features. The protagonist deciding to act (sign up, start, try) is the conversion beat. Keep characterization to a single believable detail; you have no time for backstory.

## 3. Conflict — Identify Your Compelling Conflict (the engine)

Conflict is the engine; the central question "how will this turn out?" is the only reason anyone watches. **Validate the conflict at the logline stage BEFORE building anything.** Work top-down: **logline → outline → script**, never idea → script. Spend **90% of effort on structure/outline, 10% in the writing.** Weak structure can never be fixed with better dialogue.

Define the conflict by filling out (loose brainstorm, get it all on paper):
- [ ] **Protagonist** (a short descriptor)
- [ ] **Wants / goal**
- [ ] **Action(s)** taken to get it
- [ ] **Obstacle(s) / forces of antagonism** — must be in **direct opposition** to the want
- [ ] **Stakes** — what total loss / failure looks like
- [ ] *(optional)* a **conflicting task** that goes against their nature

Three conflict layers (features have all; short pieces often only have external, which is fine):
- **Internal** — within the character (competing desires/values)
- **Interpersonal** — between characters
- **Extra-personal** — vs. the larger world/institutions

Not every protagonist needs internal conflict — pure external bulldozers (James Bond, Die Hard, Titanic = "boat sinks") work. The conflict doesn't have to sound genius; it just has to be **compelling to you**.

**→ For a short-form ad/reel:** Find the prospect's *real* conflict — usually a want blocked by a believable obstacle or objection ("wants to write a novel BUT freezes at the blank page"). That tension IS the ad. Pick ONE conflict layer (usually internal-want vs. external-obstacle). Write the logline first and gut-check: if the conflict doesn't make *you* lean in, the ad will sag no matter how slick the footage.

## 4. Stories Humans Psychologically Crave — The Story Circle (8 steps)

Dan Harmon's Story Circle (built on Joseph Campbell / the collective unconscious). Cross-culture, cross-era humans crave these same patterns — leveraging them raises the odds the work resonates. Scales from a 3-film series down to a single 30-second beat.

A circle split in half: **top hemisphere = Ordinary World**, **bottom = Special World**. **Left side = reactive/adapting**; **right side = taking control.** All beats still connect by cause-and-effect (but/therefore), not "and then."

1. **Comfort zone** — character in their familiar/ordinary world
2. **Want** — but they want something (opens the loop)
3. **Enter the unfamiliar** — cross the threshold into the special world
4. **Adapt** — struggle and adjust to it (reactive)
5. **Get what they wanted** — the goal is reached (now taking control)
6. **Pay a heavy price** — it costs them
7. **Return** — back to the familiar situation
8. **Changed** — they come back transformed (close the loop)

**Track value change:** note the **opening value** and confirm the **closing value is its opposite** (e.g., wealth−→wealth+, never-adventured→adventured). No change = nothing happened = not a story. Also confirm **forces of antagonism are in direct opposition** to the want across the whole arc.

**→ For a short-form ad/reel:** Run the full circle in 15–45s — top half is the setup (steps 1–3), bottom half is the transformation (4–8). The middle of an ad sags when it lingers in steps 3–4 (adapting) without crossing to step 5 (taking control). **Force the move to step 5 fast** — show the turn from struggling-with-the-problem to using-the-product/getting-the-win early. Opening value = the pain state; closing value = the transformed state; the product is the bridge between them.

## 5. Logline Formula — One-Sentence Hook

A logline is a 1–2 sentence summary that hooks by communicating the **central conflict** so the viewer wonders "how will this turn out?" Doubles as the ad's hook, the headline, the elevator pitch. Test it on people before building — strong response = green light.

**Formula A — basic / external (best for short pieces, no internal conflict):**
> `[Inciting incident] + [protagonist] + [action] + [antagonist] + [stakes]`

**Formula B — against-the-nature (most powerful; needs internal conflict):**
> `[Protagonist] + [want] + must [take an action that goes AGAINST their nature] + [stakes]`
> The against-nature task is the most intense conflict in the fewest words.

**Build steps for B:** (1) describe the character (e.g., lonely, violent, shy) → (2) their social role/relationship (ex-cop, gamer, son) → (3) a goal aligned with their nature → (4) a task that is the exact opposite of their nature.

**Worked examples:**
- *Speed* (A): "A young police officer must keep a bomb from exploding on a bus packed with passengers by keeping its speed above 50 mph."
- *Die Hard* (A): "A NYC cop visits LA to win back his estranged wife — but the company she works for is taken hostage by terrorists, and he may be the only one who can save them."
- Against-nature (B): "A recovering alcoholic wants to get clean, but has to become a bartender to support his sick, dying mother."

**→ For a short-form ad/reel:** Write the logline first — it IS your hook line and your brief in one sentence. For a course/product, Formula B is gold: `[prospect] wants [result] but must [face the thing they avoid] or [lose what matters]`. Example for a fiction-writing course: "An aspiring novelist with a hundred unfinished drafts wants to publish a book — but must finish something terrible first before she can ever write something great." If the one-liner doesn't hook a stranger, the ad won't either.

## 6. Scene Design — Build a Scene (5 steps)

Rule above all: **"No scene that doesn't turn."** Every scene is a *turning point* — the value at stake flips (+→− or −→+). A flat scene is almost always smuggled exposition; cut it or hide the exposition inside a scene that *does* turn (bury it in an argument).

A **beat** = one action by the protagonist + the world's reaction. The gap between expected and actual result forces a *bigger* next action.

- [ ] **Step 1 — Define conflict.** Protagonist's want as an *uninflected verb* ("to get approval," "to prepare") = the scene objective. List forces of antagonism (what they want instead / how they block). Confirm: directly opposed? If not, fix.
- [ ] **Step 2 — Opening value + charge.** What's at stake (life, wealth, love, trust…) and is it + or −?
- [ ] **Step 3 — Break into beats.** For each beat label BOTH:
  - **Surface action** — what they appear to be doing
  - **Subtextual action** — what they're really doing, as an **active gerund** (-ing): convincing, questioning, pleading, berating, seducing, prodding. Pair protagonist action with antagonist reaction.
- [ ] **Step 4 — Closing value + charge.** Must be flipped from the opening. Same charge at end = nothing happened = not a scene.
- [ ] **Step 5 — Locate the turning point + check rising action.** Each beat must require **more willpower / higher risk** than the last (easy → medium → high action). The value flips on the **last beat**. Any beats *after* the flip are not part of the scene — cut them.

Subtext bonus: the gerunds become *actor directions* ("plead with him," "berate her") — actionable, unlike un-actable states ("you're sad").

**→ For a short-form ad/reel:** An ad is usually ONE scene — so it must turn. Opening value = negative (the pain/problem); the turn flips it positive (the solution lands). Beats = micro escalation: each line/shot raises stakes toward the flip. **This is the core anti-sag tool: the middle drags whenever beats stop escalating or you pause to explain.** Keep willpower rising every beat and **cut everything after the turn** — once the value flips to positive, the ad is over; trailing beats are dead air. Aim for ~2–4 escalating beats max in 30s.

## 7. Scriptwriting — Convert Outline to a Filmable Script

Only the **last 10%** of effort. Heavy lifting (beats, structure) is already done; dialogue is "sprinkles on top," driven by the beats. If the dialogue is bad, the structure is bad — not the words.

**Core rules:**
- **Write only what can be filmed.** No un-filmable interiority ("has a flair for the unusual"). Camera-observable only.
- **Present tense** for all action.
- **Subtext, always.** "If the scene is about what the scene is about, you're in deep shit." Characters rarely state true intent; surface dialogue masks the gerund objective. (Guy doesn't say "want to marry?" — he says "you dropped this.")
- **Sound off, still makes sense.** It's a visual blueprint, not a novel.
- **Minimal parentheticals** — 2–3 per page MAX; overuse = crutch for weak structure and stepping on the actor.

**Five components:**
1. **Slug line** — `INT./EXT. + LOCATION + TIME OF DAY` (all caps; auto). Use `INT./EXT.` for in/out scenes like cars. Tells production location/scheduling/lighting.
2. **Action** — present tense, observable. First appearance of a character in ALL CAPS.
3. **Character name** — ALL CAPS, centered above dialogue. (Avoid same-letter names — confusing.)
4. **Dialogue** — driven by the beat's gerund objective.
5. **Parenthetical** — sparse; a verb action or inflection note (e.g., "deadpan," "singing," a needed pause) only when the script can't otherwise convey it.

**Character intro** = the one place you may break "filmable only": ALL CAPS first mention, ≤1–2 sentences, physical AND emotional (sets the frame for hair/makeup/wardrobe/casting). Tags: `(O.S.)` off-screen but present, `(V.O.)` voice-over not in scene, `(CONT'D)` auto at breaks. Tools: Highland 2 (free) or any equivalent; work in 25/5 Pomodoro sprints. Get feedback only from people who make films (they can read an outline); not random viewers.

**→ For a short-form ad/reel:** Script is a tight shot-driven blueprint — every line must survive with the sound off (most viewers watch muted), so carry the story in visuals + on-screen text, not VO exposition. Keep dialogue subtextual and short; the CTA is the resolution beat, not a tacked-on tag. One slug line, 2–4 beats, ruthless cuts after the turn.

## Fast 60-Second Brief Checklist

- [ ] One-sentence **logline** that makes a stranger ask "how does this turn out?" (Formula B if there's an internal conflict)
- [ ] **Active protagonist** = the prospect; events caused BY their choice (Decision Test)
- [ ] **One compelling conflict**, antagonist in direct opposition; **Risk Test** passes
- [ ] Beats connected by **BUT / THEREFORE**, stakes **escalating** every beat
- [ ] Opening value (pain, −) **flips** to closing value (win, +) — the **turn**
- [ ] **Cut everything after the turn**; force the move to "taking control" early to kill middle-sag
- [ ] Sound-off legible; subtext over stated intent; CTA = the resolution beat
