# The Director's Eye

> Purpose: The director's mindset and a runnable intent checklist for raising the quality of any brief or shot list — usable by a non-director before generating or assembling clips.

## What a Director Actually Is

The director is the AUTHOR (auteur) of the piece, because the medium tells its story through PICTURES, not words. The script/brief is a blueprint — a master plan, not the finished art (like architectural plans vs. the building). Authorship lives in how images are chosen and sequenced.

- A director tells the story through "a succession of juxtaposed images." So the director's #1 job is deciding WHERE THE CAMERA GOES (in AI terms: what each shot frames and how shots sequence) — because that is how meaning is made.
- This is NOT the cinematographer's job. The DP owns light and the look of the image and may suggest camera ideas — but the director makes the final call based on WHY the camera goes there, because the director owns the story.
- Directing actors / performance / look are all SUPPORT for the main job: positioning images to move the story forward in the audience's mind. Don't confuse "managing the talent" with directing.
- Practical reframe for AI video: you are not "describing a cool scene." You are authoring meaning by choosing what each shot contains and what it sits next to.

## Serve the Audience, Not the Ego

Directing is a TECHNICAL SKILL, like piano — learnable by anyone through study, practice, and commitment. Genius/talent only makes learning faster; it is helpful, not required. This is the unlock: quality comes from craft and good questions, not innate gift.

- Separate your self-worth from the work's quality. Pre-1700s, genius was seen as an EXTERNAL force (a muse/spirit working through you). Borrow that view: if a piece fails, "the genius wasn't with me today," not "I'm worthless." It protects your judgment under criticism (even Spielberg can't read his reviews).
- Ego corrupts decisions. When you decide to look cool or be liked, you usually make the OPPOSITE choice from what serves the audience — which makes the work worse, which makes your image worse. Caring about image backfires.
- The tell: "What's the coolest place to put the camera?" is the ego question. "What does the story need here?" is the craft question. Ask the craft one.
- TWO master questions answer every creative decision and every crew/tool question:
  1. **What is the meaning of THIS scene/shot?**
  2. **What is the story ABOUT?**
  If a choice doesn't serve one of these, it's an ego choice — cut it.

## Win Under Pressure

Things WILL go wrong; that's normal, not a verdict on you. "In filmmaking there are no problems, only solutions" — reframe every obstacle as a solution waiting to be found.

- Struggle = growth signal. Rather be the worst player in the NFL (surrounded by, and pushed by, excellence) than the best in a weak league. Discomfort means you're in the room that will level you up.
- Failure is not personal worth. When results lag, it's because you're growing and you have the tools to solve it — not because you're a bad artist. Expect the hard moment so it doesn't knock you over.
- Tools + structure don't replace the work. The map saves you years and money, but you still have to walk the road. Commitment beats talent.
- Storytelling is leverage everywhere — selling, teaching, persuading, relationships — not just film. Every ad is a story being told to serve a viewer.

## Director's Intent Checklist

Run this against any brief, shot list, or batch of clips BEFORE generating or assembling. If an item fails, fix or cut.

### 1. Intent (run first, on the whole piece)
- [ ] What is the story ABOUT? (one sentence)
- [ ] What should the audience FEEL — and what should they DO after?
- [ ] What is the VALUE SHIFT? Which emotional state do we open on, which do we land on? (e.g. pain → relief)
- [ ] Who is the audience, and what do they need from this — not what do I want to show off?

### 2. Per shot / per clip
- [ ] What is this shot FOR? What single beat (verb) does it carry?
- [ ] Does it MOVE the story forward, or just repeat a beat we already have? (Cut repeats.)
- [ ] Is it absolutely NECESSARY right now? If not — omit it, or save it for when it becomes necessary.
- [ ] Am I trying to ESTABLISH (setting, character, backstory)? If yes, stop — start later, show action instead.
- [ ] Is the shot simple/uninflected enough that the CUT can do the work? (Resist the all-in-one mega-shot.)
- [ ] Does meaning come from this shot's NEIGHBORS, or am I overloading the single shot?

### 3. Ego vs. service gut-check
- [ ] Am I choosing this to look cool / be liked, or to serve the story and audience?
- [ ] Does every choice trace back to "meaning of the scene" or "what the story is about"?
- [ ] Am I drawing attention to ME (the filmmaker / clever camera) instead of to the story?
- [ ] Am I relying on "performance" to carry emotion the CUT should be carrying?

### 4. Sequence / assembly
- [ ] Do we open at the latest, hottest moment — intrigue before orientation?
- [ ] Does each beat advance toward the objective (verb → verb → verb)?
- [ ] Does the order produce the intended emotion via juxtaposition (see montage-kuleshov.md)?
- [ ] Does it END on the value/feeling we want the audience to act on?

### 5. Pressure / process
- [ ] When something breaks, am I asking "what's the solution?" instead of stalling on "this is a problem"?
- [ ] Am I separating the work's quality from my self-worth so I can judge it clearly?
- [ ] Am I doing the work the craft requires, not waiting on talent or inspiration?

## One-Line Operating Creed

Author the meaning by choosing and sequencing images; answer every decision with "what's the meaning / what's it about"; serve the audience over the ego; treat problems as solutions.
